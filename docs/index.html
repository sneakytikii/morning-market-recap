The week, in review지난 한 주 돌아보기

A week of very loud news added up to almost nothing on the surface. The S&P 500 finished at 7,718.60, up 0.09% for the week — nine hundredths of one percent, after five sessions that held an oil scare, a worldwide sell-off in government bonds, one Fed official who moved the rate odds with a single sentence, and the strongest jobs report in months. Underneath, the week split cleanly in two: chips ran, shops fell. Nvidia rose about 5.9%, the triple-speed chip fund SOXL about 5.3% and QQQ about 0.35% — while Costco fell about 3.1%, down in every one of the five sessions, in the same week it reported August sales up nearly 10%.

아주 시끄러웠던 한 주가 겉으로는 거의 아무것도 아닌 것으로 끝났습니다. S&P 500은 7,718.60으로 마쳐 한 주 동안 0.09% 올랐습니다. 1%의 100분의 9입니다. 그 닷새 안에는 유가 불안, 세계적인 국채 매도, 문장 하나로 금리 확률을 움직인 연준 인사, 그리고 몇 달 만에 가장 강한 고용 보고서가 다 있었습니다. 그 아래에서 한 주는 둘로 깨끗이 갈렸습니다. 반도체는 달렸고 가게는 내렸습니다. 엔비디아는 약 5.9%, 3배속 반도체 펀드 SOXL은 약 5.3%, QQQ는 약 0.35% 올랐습니다. 반면 코스트코는 약 3.1% 내렸습니다. 8월 매출이 10% 가까이 늘었다고 발표한 바로 그 주에, 닷새 모두 하락했습니다.

The bigger story is still interest rates, and this week it turned twice. On Thursday, Fed governor Christopher Waller said he would be “inclined to support” leaving rates alone this month unless the inflation figures surprise; the market's measure of the odds of a September rise fell from about 63% to about 50%, and stocks had their best day in a month. On Friday morning the August jobs report came in at 162,000 new jobs against the 55,000 or so expected — strong hiring is read here as a reason to keep rates high — and the odds went straight back to about 60%. Long-term borrowing costs ended the week higher, the 10-year government rate near 4.78%, and oil is dearer again at about $91 a barrel after tankers were hit near the Strait of Hormuz. Nobody can tell you what the Fed will do on September 16.

The week ahead is short and it ends with the number that matters. Monday is Labor Day and the market is closed; producer prices arrive Thursday, September 10, and the August inflation report on Friday, September 11, at 5:30 in the morning Pacific time — the last big figure before the Fed decides on September 16, and Fed officials stopped speaking publicly on Saturday, so nobody there will say another word before the vote. Two standing risks do not change. Reasons it could go up

The AI buildout stopped being an argument this week and started being an order book: Dell disclosed a record $95 billion of AI server orders it has not yet filled, Broadcom's AI chip sales rose 221% in a year, and the memory makers that supply them are reported sold out into next year. That is real revenue from real customers, and it is what the index's biggest companies sell. The economy keeps testing well too — 162,000 new jobs in August, roughly three times what was expected, with the prior two months revised up. The fear gauge never left the low-to-mid 14s all week, which is what a calm market looks like from the inside. And the rate pressure has a dated counterweight: the Treasury's doubled buying of long-term debt begins Wednesday, September 9.

Reasons it could go down

A rate rise on September 16 is back to the better-than-even reading — about 60% on one common measure after Friday's jobs report — and this time no Fed official can talk it back down, because they have all stopped speaking until after the vote. Long-term borrowing costs finished the week higher on both ends, the 10-year near 4.78% and the 30-year near 5.25%, and Tuesday showed how fast that can turn unpleasant: Japan's long-term rate crossed 3% for the first time since 1996 and the whole world's bonds sold off together. Oil is the newest push behind it, near $91 a barrel after attacks on tankers near the Strait of Hormuz. Underneath the flat index the average company had a poor week — the money went to a handful of chip names and came out of almost everything else. And the trade fight starts costing money on September 8, when Canada taxes more than 700 American products.
